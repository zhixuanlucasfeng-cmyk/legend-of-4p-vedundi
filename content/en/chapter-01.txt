Vedundi could not remember how he had arrived there.

That was the first wrong thing.

Dreams never tell you they are dreams while they last. They simply withhold a single fact, carve out a hollow space in your memory, and let you build an entire coherent world around the empty gap you did not know existed. You walk inside the fiction unquestioningly, following rules you never learned, trusting logic that was never complete, and only after everything collapses do you realize how deeply you were fooled.

He knew the street was Bangkok. He knew it in the way you know things only in dreams — no evidence, no observation, no logical inference. It was the same pre‑knowledge a person holds before they are taught their own name: pure, unarguable, rooted in someplace deeper than consciousness.

Behind him, tiered golden rooftops stacked upward, sinking softly into the hazy tropical mist. The Grand Palace stood close enough to touch, close enough for any ordinary tourist to wander over in a few casual steps, close enough to feel the weight of its sacred, glittering grandeur.

He did not walk toward the gold.

He turned instead into the narrow alley beside the palace, the old shadowed strip tucked away from all the brightness and crowds. It looked forgotten, marginal, insignificant beside the royal architecture, yet it carried a quiet certainty: this part of the city had always been waiting for him, specifically, patiently, through every unspoken night of his life.

A wooden sign creaked overhead, swaying gently in the damp wind. It had the exaggerated, meticulous texture unique to fully realized dreams — every crack, every faded stroke of paint, every loose screw was present, detailed perfectly for no real purpose other than completion.

Three languages lined the board. He read all three effortlessly.

Dreams were always generous with comprehension.

The words printed plain and unadorned in the center of the sign: Sex Reassignment Surgery.

He walked inside without making a decision.

No hesitation, no doubt, no internal debate. His body moved on its own, following a script already written in the dark of his subconscious. The door closed behind him, sealing off the warm humid air of the street and replacing it with cold, dry, antiseptic stillness.

Someone measured his frame.

No tape measure touched his skin. Hands moved over his shoulders, his torso, his limbs, gauging every contour with precise, impersonal accuracy. No one spoke to him. No one asked for consent. No one confirmed a single preference.

A figure wrote on a clipboard. The tip of the pen scratched steadily across the surface.

A number appeared on the page: four.

He did not recall speaking it aloud. He had no memory of nodding, no memory of agreeing, no memory of acknowledging the figure or the question. Yet the number remained, fixed and final, as though it had been submitted by him and confirmed in his name.

Beneath the number, handwriting unfolded across the paper. The strokes resembled his own and yet were alien, disciplined in a way he never was, calm in a way he could never manage. One word was written down, then a second, a third, a fourth.

They lined up in a neat vertical column, itemized and accounted for, like charges on a bill he had never ordered but was expected to pay in full.

Some part of him, the part that had spent eighteen years silently signing off on every uncomfortable compromise, every unearned shame, every quiet humiliation just to make the staring rooms stop watching him, had already signed the receipt in his place.

No one asked if he was sure.

In dreams, certainty is not a question. It is a delivery. Your wants, your sacrifices, your unspoken bargains arrive already signed, already sealed, already enforced before you know you have agreed.

The surgical lights descended in a flat, blinding sheet of pure white.

Hands moved across his body. He never saw the faces behind the masks, only the gloved fingers working methodically, the quiet motion of professionals performing routine labor. No emotion, no malice, no sympathy. Just work.

A machine hummed in three steady beats.

Even. Patient. Indifferent to what it counted.

Then the light changed color.

Surgical lights do not change color. It is not permitted. It is not possible in the ordered logic of the waking world.

Some primitive, sleeping part of his body understood this before his mind did. A raw, animal alertness woke inside him, recognizing anomaly long before reason could formulate fear.

The second awakening was worse than the first.

The first awakening could end the dream. This one trapped him inside it.

He woke on the operating table.

He was conscious. Fully, brutally conscious. He could feel the open space in his body, the way his flesh had been accessed and rearranged. He was being processed like a parcel, opened and sorted, its contents removed and reallocated without permission. Whatever belonged to him was no longer his to keep.

"One kidney," a voice said pleasantly.

It did not speak to him. It spoke to the empty air, to the room, to the invisible system running the operation.

"One is enough. No need for two."

He lowered his head to look.

His neck should not have moved. His body should have been immobilized. None of this should have been possible. But nightmares obey only one rule: your flesh will cooperate exactly enough to let you witness every loss inflicted on it.

A hollow space existed inside him now, warm and newly vacant.

On the steel tray beside him lay a single kidney. Small, dark, ordinary. Ridiculously, humiliatingly ordinary. It looked like nothing more than a discarded item, unremarkable, disposable.

No one asked his permission for this either.

He heard his scream before he felt it rise.

The sound tore out of him without negotiation. It was not a question. It was not a plea for bargaining. He understood instantly that there was no one here to bargain with. These people were not tormentors. They were staff. They had been assigned their roles long before his birth.

"I don't want to die!"

"I just want to live!"

"That is all I ask for!"

The dream shattered.

He jolted back into his bed, soaked through with cold sweat. The hour was three in the morning, the dead quiet center of the night where no one witnesses your collapse.

The three‑beat hum followed him into reality.

He realized slowly, numbly, that it was the old air conditioner compressor, cycling on and off year after year, never serviced, never repaired, loyal to its broken rhythm longer than any person had ever been loyal to him.

He sat up.

His legs held him upright. It was a tiny, private miracle, but he had no strength left to be grateful for it.

He walked to the bathroom, turned on the faucet, and submerged both hands under the running water. He kept them there until the cold stopped feeling cold, until his skin turned numb and unresponsive. He washed his face twice, watching himself in the mirror the entire time.

He looked at his own reflection as if monitoring a stranger he had been entrusted to guard.

He counted his breaths.

In four counts. Hold four counts. Out four counts.

He had read somewhere that disciplined rhythm could stabilize a frayed mind.

He followed the method strictly.

In theory, a lie repeated with enough discipline will eventually harden into truth.

Nine seconds passed.

Nothing healed. Nothing settled.

Then the bathroom door opened.

No knock.

This door had never been knocked on in his life.

His grandmother filled the doorway the way weather fills a window — entirely, unannounced, already in progress before you notice it has arrived.

"You," she said. "What are you doing awake in the middle of the night."

He had no time to answer. The question was never meant to be answered. It was an opening statement for judgment.

In her hand was a phone charging cable, coiled tightly once around her fist in a motion practiced by years of repetition.

She snapped her wrist.

The cord struck his shoulder and the side of his neck. The sound was thick and flat, like a wet towel slapping cold tile.

He did not flinch.

He had trained the flinch out of himself long ago, like sanding away a splinter until the wood no longer registers the abrasion. That total, mechanical compliance angered her more than any resistance ever could.

"Drop out," she said, breath shallow from the effort. "You fat pig. You weren't sleeping anyway. There's no point in studying."

She left the room without turning off the light.

The brightness stayed nailed to the walls, a final unspoken sentence: you are not worth the darkness. No one needs to consider which way you might fall.

Vedundi stood in the bathroom for a long time.

Water dripped from his chin and fingertips onto the floor. A bruise was blooming beneath his collarbone, slow and quiet. By morning it would settle into a shape almost like a signature.

He thought one thing, clear and unshaken beneath all the pain.

I only asked for four.

He did not yet know what he had actually received. Nor did he know who had been listening all along.
