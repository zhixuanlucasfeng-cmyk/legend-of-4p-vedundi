Twenty-Seven.

The sky had not fully brightened when Vedundi woke up.

It was not a natural awakening. It was a jolt pulled upward from the bottom of his body. His senses woke before his mind, confirming one cruel truth: the surgical lamp from his dream was gone, but the debt it left behind remained.

The bruise below his collarbone had darkened overnight. It was no longer a hot, vivid purple, but a bruised black rimmed with dull ochre, its boundary sharp and precise, as if traced by a ruler. Permanent. Unavoidable.

He stood before the mirror and pulled his collar half an inch higher until the mark sank completely into shadow. The movement was muscle memory. He had hidden wounds too many times to need sight to guide his hands.

Breakfast was a cold leftover slice of toast. Dry, tasteless, lifeless. His grandmother's bedroom door stayed shut. No sound escaped it. He passed it without pause, without detour, as if walking past a wall.

Walls do not hit you. But they do not promise safety either.

The school bus arrived at eight sharp.

He boarded slowly, without hurry or delay, and took his usual seat by the window in the second-to-last row. He placed his backpack on his knees, upright and orderly, exactly as he did every morning.

Then his phone vibrated in his pocket.

Once. Again. Then continuously, a swarming, endless hum, like a whole nest of bees trapped against his thigh, buzzing through the fabric, numbing his skin.

He pulled out the phone. Messages flooded the screen.

Seventeen questions, all wrapped in the soft pretense of innocent curiosity.

Are you and Bruhan really together?

We just want the truth.

Adam said you two were in the bathroom. Is it true?

None of them asked if he wanted to answer. Politeness in questions only benefits the asker, never the asked.

His thumb hovered over the input box. He had replies ready. He wanted to clarify that none of the rumors were true, that the word forced had been scripted for him, spoken by others, never by him.

After a long second, he pulled his finger away. The screen dimmed. Nothing was typed. Nothing was explained.

It was not surrender. It was arithmetic.

Seventeen people with seventeen versions of "just curious" did not want the truth. They wanted participation. To answer would only give them content to forward, to spread, to multiply into the eighteenth, nineteenth, twentieth iteration of the lie.

He scrolled further down.

Ten more messages. No questions. Only periods. Only commands.

Stay away from Bruhan.

Try him again and see what happens.

This is your final warning.

Seventeen inquiries. Ten threats.

Twenty-seven.

A clean, final number. Four surgeries in the dream. Twenty-seven judgments in reality. A person's ledger never runs out of space. There is always room for one more line of debt.

He locked the phone and slipped it back into his pocket. He did not reply to a single one. There was no point in defending himself to a crowd that had already declared him guilty.

The bus rolled forward. Sand-colored buildings slid backward outside the window. His reflection blurred across the glass, the same eyes that had opened on the surgical table the night before — empty, tired, unchanged.

When the morning bell rang, he was already seated in his usual desk, second-to-last row. A seat no one else ever bothered to take.

Whispers lingered behind him, fragmented and intentional, pitched just loud enough for him to hear and just quiet enough for everyone else to feign ignorance.

Mr. Pepper called names one by one. When he reached Vedundi Singh, he paused for half a second.

A half-second longer than normal. Not enough to be noticeable to the room, but enough for Vedundi to feel it.

It was the only adult acknowledgment of what was happening to him all day. No follow-up. No conversation. No intervention. Just half a second, gone as quickly as it came.

Later, in the staff office, Mr. Pepper would write Vedundi's name on a referral form alongside other students. The form would be filed away, unread. It was the smallest, most powerless kindness he could offer — doing something empty was better than doing nothing at all.

During break, Vedundi walked to the water fountain. Passing the basketball court corridor, he saw Bruhan.

Bruhan stood centered in a group of friends, twirling a pen between his fingers, laughing easily, shoulders relaxed, completely untouched by the storm circulating around Vedundi's name.

He did not look over. He may not have noticed Vedundi passing by. Or he may have noticed and chosen to ignore it.

Vedundi did not slow down, did not speed up. He walked past five meters behind the group like passing a shop window displaying something he had once been allowed to touch, now labeled Not For Sale.

His chest hollowed out. Not a sharp pain that bends you over, but a soft, quiet absence, like a sheet of paper being pulled silently from your grasp — lighter afterward, emptier.

He filled his water, drank two sips, returned his bottle, and walked back to class.

Lunch filled the cafeteria with noise and clatter. Groups clustered at every table, loud and alive.

Vedundi carried his tray through the rows, searching for a corner that was not yet occupied and would not immediately empty when he sat down. He had mastered this search over years — finding the weakest signal in a fully connected world.

He sat alone at the far end of a long window table. The bench seated six easily. Within minutes, it seated only him.

Two junior boys approached with full trays, saw who was sitting there, and turned away without hesitation. The movement was practiced, automatic, instinctive avoidance.

He ate slowly, mechanically, without tasting anything. Outside the window, basketballs thudded against hoops, a distant rhythm belonging to another, brighter world.

He finished, cleared his tray, and returned to his studies.

During afternoon break, he finished two-thirds of his homework. Not out of diligence, but because solving problems was one of the few ways he could prove to himself that he was still functioning, still breathing, still here. Surviving stares and finishing equations cost him the same quiet strength.

The final bell rang. The crowd flooded toward the school gate.

Vedundi moved with the current, steady as a stone in flowing water. People parted around him without thought.

At the school entrance, the line froze.

Not everyone. A localized, uncanny stillness.

A girl's sentence died mid-breath. A boy's hand hung suspended in the air before his friend's shoulder. Upperclassmen turned simultaneously, then pretended to look elsewhere.

No one shouted his name. No one pointed. The silence was coordinated, polite, devastating. The entire student body had agreed upon a clean, dignified erasure.

A quiet scoff escaped someone's nose. Eyes glued to him despite obvious shame, watching the wreckage like a traffic accident you cannot look away from.

Some mouths curved into faint, cruel smiles — not amusement, but casual entertainment. He was today's spectacle.

Most people sidestepped half a step, reshaping the queue naturally, as if a forbidden line had always existed where he stood.

The world continued outside this bubble. The evening bell rang. Street food stalls smoked and sizzled. Life moved forward.

Only his small patch of space stopped entirely — too quiet, too clean, so clean that even mercy felt unnecessary.

Vedundi kept walking.

A girl ahead of him lost grip of her stack of handouts. Chemistry review sheets spilled across the pavement.

The surrounding crowd watched. Nobody moved.

Vedundi knelt and gathered the pages one by one, smoothed the creases, realigned the stack neatly, and held it out.

The girl's face panicked when she realized who had helped her. She took the papers quickly, whispered a nearly inaudible "thank you," avoided his eyes, and fled back into the crowd as if kindness toward him was contagious, as if gratitude would stain her.

He stood there for two seconds, watching her retreat.

That single thank you was the only unconditioned warmth he had received all day. Pure, unguarded, free of suspicion.

It lasted less than two seconds before she withdrew it entirely, erasing the small light she had given.

He did not blame her. He expected it.

The dream bill marked with the number four had never truly vanished after waking. It had simply changed form.

No longer surgical incisions, but twenty-seven cruel messages, a whole crowd's synchronized avoidance, unspoken accusations, and a fleeting warmth snatched away before it could settle.

Through every line of this new ledger, he had never once written the word hate.

He walked home slower than usual. Not from exhaustion, but from deliberate delay. The later he returned, the higher the chance his grandmother would be asleep. It was the only variable he could control in his favor.

The corridor lights had been broken for months. He climbed the dark stairs by counting steps alone. Once he had misstepped and stood trapped in the blackness for a long time, refusing to turn on his flashlight, afraid the slit of light beneath the door would announce his presence.

A thin yellow line seeped from under his front door.

The living room light remained on, sterile and unforgiving.

He gripped the doorknob and paused, recounting the day's accounts: twenty-seven judgments at dawn, silent exclusion at noon, unspoken explanations left to rot, and one brief warmth retracted.

He chose not to count the thank you as debt.

His phone vibrated again.

Twenty-eighth message. Unknown number.

You deserve this.

No context, no discussion, no trial. Final verdict.

He did not reply. In the black screen, his reflection stared back — calm, detached, like a stranger's face.

He pushed the door open.

Silence. Grandmother's door closed.

Relief washed over him immediately, followed by sharp shame. A person should never feel grateful for simply not being hurt that day.

He set down his backpack, sat on the edge of his bed, and pulled out his frayed, cheap notebook — the only thing he owned entirely for himself.

He flipped to today's date.

His pen moved steadily across the blank page.

27

No commentary. No emotion. No justification. Only the number, sitting alone on the line, a quiet archive of all he had endured.

He closed the notebook, turned off the light, and lay down.

The old water stain on the ceiling remained exactly where it had been the night before, unchanged, permanent.

He stared at it, letting his breathing slow until its rhythm matched the three-beat cycle of the surgical machine from his dream.

Loop after loop.

No escape.
