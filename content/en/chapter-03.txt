The moment he closed his notebook, every sound in the room fell into a precise, heavy rhythm.

The air conditioner's monotonous hum, the distant continuous roar of traffic, and his steadying breath wove together, replicating the cold cycle of surgical instruments in his dream. Silent, unchanging, endless. It reminded him that no suffering truly ended. It was only archived, stored in the depths of his bones, waiting to resurface and drown him again.

The lonely number 27 lay quiet on the page, submerged in night.

Twenty-seven messages, twenty-seven unjust verdicts, twenty-seven layers of cold that seeped into his marrow. These invisible wounds settled deeper than the bruise beneath his collarbone, heavier and far harder to heal.

He had thought this was all the fate would hand him today.

Until darkness swallowed every corner of the room, and that cold unfamiliar text rose from the back of his mind — you deserve it — marking a brand-new, inescapable count.

Twenty-eight.

He lay flat, eyes closed, no struggle, no resistance. Thick darkness wrapped around him, leaving no room for grief or anger. Only a barren, trampled numbness remained. When a person is worn down to the core, tears do not come. They only remember, clear and brutal, every detail of what destroyed them.

Three years ago, nothing terrible had happened.

There had been no scene, no mistake, no wrongdoing.

There had only been a rumor — fabricated, groundless, and deadly.

That was the cruelty of it. Rumors required no evidence, no witnesses, no truth. They only needed one person to speak, one person to spread it, and a crowd willing to believe. Then innocence was buried forever.

June seventh, 2023. Second period break.

Second-floor corridor of the Palace Building. Soft daylight stretched across the floor. Students wandered and chatted, unaware that a few casual guesses would forge a chain that bound his youth in perpetual torment.

Human memory was fickle. People forgot gentle moments, precious days, small kindnesses. But they never forgot the exact moment they were pushed into despair.

He was eighteen then, a final-year student at Procoloco College, crushed under four heavy A-Level courses. His days were rigid, dull, and predictable. Bruhan carried only three subjects, seemingly free, yet trapped in a heavier cage. His father's business lived and died by reputation. Fame was not luxury, but survival. The family name could not bear the slightest stain.

Free periods were their only escape. Every corner of the school was ruled by schedules and supervision, except the quiet corridors and empty window ledges. In those unguarded gaps, they had shared a secret, pure, undisturbed tenderness.

A tenderness the world refused to tolerate.

That day, they had only stood side by side by the window, speaking softly, sharing a quiet understanding no one else could perceive. Nothing more. No boundaries crossed, no rules broken, no shame deserved.

Yet Johnny Ansari raised his phone.

He hid in the shadow of the corridor corner, still and silent, recording several seconds of footage without a sound.

Looking back, what chilled Vedundi most was not the act of filming, but Johnny's complete indifference. The whole school laughed at every small embarrassment — spilled drinks, wrong answers, clumsy steps. But Johnny did not laugh. No smirk, no amusement, no casual curiosity.

Four seconds of cold, steady, certain recording. He was not capturing a trivial moment. He was archiving a sentence.

When he finished, he turned and walked straight to the vice-principal's office, without hesitation.

Mr. Pepper was tired and desensitized, his empathy long replaced by rigid school regulations. That day he delivered hollow justice: he forced Johnny to delete the video on the spot and watched the recycle bin empty completely.

The footage vanished. But the rumor had already broken free, spreading across the entire school like wildfire.

Files could be erased. Suspicion, gossip, and malice could not. The school erased the evidence but allowed the slander to root permanently.

Suspension came swiftly and arbitrarily. One week for both of them. No investigation, no explanation, no chance for the truth to surface. The school quieted public chaos with punishment and left private hatred to grow unchecked.

Frank told him the truth later. On the same afternoon, Adam Khalifa cornered Johnny by the lockers, demanding an Airdrop of the deleted video.

Johnny told him plainly that it was gone, erased under Pepper's direct supervision.

Adam only replied coldly, "Useless." The tone was light, yet utterly dismissive, discarding Johnny like a broken tool. Johnny shrugged and walked away. No one asked for the truth. The crowd only wanted a story.

The suspension ended, but the whispers never stopped. Isolation and prejudice sank deep into the school's atmosphere.

Bruhan changed completely.

Vedundi once thought his cruelty was inherent, until he realized most cowardice was inherited. Bruhan never lived for himself. He lived under his father's pride, his family's reputation, and the public gaze. A single blemish on the name meant the collapse of his ordered life.

During PE class, before the entire year group, Bruhan cut every tie publicly, sharply, mercilessly.

He used the fatal word — forced.

He declared that Vedundi had pursued him, harassed him, ruined his life and tainted his reputation.

The class made a strange, chilling sound, halfway between gasp and laughter — the sound of a crowd granted permission to be cruel. For years afterward, that noise haunted Vedundi's quiet nights, scraping at his nerves and eroding his dignity.

No one knew that after publicly condemning Vedundi, Bruhan broke down and retched alone in the corridor corner. Aryan witnessed it but spread a false excuse of food poisoning. Everyone accepted the convenient lie. No one chose to see his guilt, his struggle, his fear.

Vedundi held that secret hatred for a year and a half. He hated Bruhan's cowardice, his betrayal, his choice to sacrifice others for his own safety. Only later did he soften, acknowledging that Bruhan was also trapped, also a victim of family pressure and public expectation — while he himself had been the easiest target.

Then Adam Khalifa stepped into the void.

Raised on stages since childhood, performing before countless audiences, Adam could never exist without attention. He craved sightlines, reactions, and spotlight in everything he did.

His persecution of Vedundi was theatrical, relentless, and public. He never insulted him directly, never spread rumors himself, never laid a hand on him. Yet he directed every wave of public malice straight toward Vedundi.

Starved of kindness for far too long, Vedundi made the most vulnerable mistake. He mistook Adam's performative warmth for genuine salvation. He thought he had finally found someone who understood him. It was only another staged performance.

The curtain fell one evening when he woke up dazed and cold on the roadside, five hundred meters from school. His phone blazed with endless notifications.

An anonymous group channel had been created. People hid behind screens, spreading mockery, body shaming, distorted gossip, and endless variations of the baseless rumor. Malice flooded in, overwhelming him completely.

Adam never posted a single insult. Such petty cruelty was beneath his persona.

But he never left the channel.

Silent observation, silent permission, silent destruction. He watched calmly as Vedundi was torn apart by the crowd.

Vedundi fainted twice that day, completely drained.

He woke in the hospital, staring at pale white ceilings, surrounded by biting sterilized air. He learned later that his sister had skipped her crucial mock exams to stay by his side, never uttering a word of her sacrifice. He only discovered it through the blank record on her report card.

Beside his bed lay an unclaimed bag: his usual order from the school gate café, cold karak chai and a cold cheese sandwich. No note, no clue, no source.

His sister blamed the nurses. The nurses blamed the family. That small, unsolved warmth remained as a faint, untouchable light in his broken memories.

He returned to school after recovering.

Everything looked the same — bells, classrooms, sunsets. Only people had changed. Every gaze was cold, distant, and unforgiving.

For a full week, Bruhan looked straight through him as if he were transparent air.

July seventh, form time.

Vedundi stepped forward to ask one simple question, no accusation, no blame, no persistence.

Before the words left his mouth, Bruhan shoved him hard and ruthlessly.

He stumbled, then rose steadily to his feet.

He always rose. Even in ruin, he refused to give the crowd the satisfaction of seeing him break. That quiet stubbornness made others uneasy, irritated, and cruel.

He stood firm in the doorway, unyielding, waiting for an answer he would never receive.

Cornered and panicked, Bruhan resorted to his deadliest weapon — public accusation.

He shouted harassment. He shouted the humiliating nickname Four P loud enough to brand it permanently into everyone's memory.

When Vedundi lunged forward in blind instinct, Bruhan screamed the lie that destroyed everything:

"He's trying to force me!"

No proof, no context, no truth. Only a lie spoken loudly enough to be universally believed.

Rifi Kari and Amman Alfie moved in instantly.

They did not hate Vedundi.

That was the darkest truth of all. Rifi fought not out of anger, but for belonging. Violence was his way of fitting in. Amman fought out of fear of Rifi, blindly following the crowd's malice.

Fear traveled down the chain. Malice traveled down the chain.

Vedundi was the final, helpless link — the designated sacrifice for everyone's cowardice and conformity.

When the chaos ended, the classroom was in shambles.

Bruhan's arm was fractured. Vedundi's face was split open, requiring stitches.

The school's judgment was cold and biased.

Three days' suspension for the others.

One week's suspension for Vedundi, plus a final warning: expulsion upon next offense.

Mr. Pepper filed mandatory counseling referrals for all four boys.

He knew no one would attend. He knew the forms were meaningless. Yet he filed them anyway — the only faint, powerless kindness the rigid rules allowed him to offer.

Back in the pitch-black bedroom, Vedundi opened his eyes.

No tears, no light, only dead stillness.

He remembered the twenty-seven counts he had written the night before — the mocking stares at the school gate, the empty cafeteria table, the fleeting thank-you he had taken back, the cold text that told him he deserved every hurt.

Then he remembered the last memory he had buried deep down, too painful to unpack.

The evening after the classroom fight.

His only way home.

No detour, no escape, no chance of luck.

They were waiting for him on that road.

He stopped the thought there.

The night wind brushed the rooftop, tried to slip into the room, then retreated gently, as if afraid to touch his brokenness.

Lying alone in darkness, staring at the faded water stain on the ceiling, he silently marked today's final debt.

Twenty-seven was the coldness of the present.

Twenty-eight was the unburned ash of the past.

And he would always have no choice but to receive, endure, and carry every last bruise life had forced upon him.
